a case study

A new model in production in under a day: how Notion evaluates and traces its AI

When a new frontier model comes out, Notion usually has it in front of users in less than a day. That is only safe if your evals answer two questions fast: what does the new model break, and what does it do better? This is how Notion's AI team says it does that, with searchable traces, narrow judges written per example, and three kinds of evals with different jobs. Three of our five sources are written by Braintrust, the vendor whose tool Notion uses, and we label them that way throughout.

By Anuj Kumar Sharma about 24 minutes Module 5 · AI Evaluation (Evals) & Observability
the story

story · 01Why this case study

Building an eval is the easy part. Keeping evals useful is harder, once your prompts run to hundreds of thousands of tokens, models change every few months, and dozens of engineers ship at once. Notion is a good example because its AI lead has spoken openly about how the team works, including the parts that aren't tidy.

You will see two ideas you can use on any project: traces you can search, so a rare failure becomes an eval row; and three kinds of evals with different pass-rate targets, so a new model can be judged in hours rather than weeks.

read this first: who wrote the sources

S1, S2 and S3 are written by Braintrust, the company that sells the evaluation tool Notion uses. They are marketing as well as reporting, and their numbers aren't independently measured. S4 and S5 are Notion's own people talking: Sarah Sachs, who leads Notion's AI modeling teams, in a 2025 conference workshop, and Sarah with co-founder Simon Last on a 2026 podcast. Both are spoken and transcribed by machine, so we quote only short, clear phrases. Notion has not published its own engineering post on evals.

what you will be able to do

Find a rare failure in long traces and turn it into an eval row, write judges that stay valid as your data changes, separate regression, launch and frontier evals, and plan a same-day model release with a go/no-go you can defend.

story · 02The problem: evals that don't keep up

Notion shipped its AI writing assistant in November 2022, AI Autofill in June 2023 and Q&A, chat over your whole workspace, in November 2023.S1 intro Evaluation started simple and manual:

  • Datasets were JSONL files in a git repo, hard to manage, version and work on together.S1 Q&A evals
  • Human evaluators scored individual responses, an expensive and slow feedback loop.S1 Q&A evals The data specialists doing it worked in Google Sheets.S4 08:48

That was enough to launch Q&A in beta in late 2023.S1 Q&A evals Then several things grew at once:

  • Agents. In 2023 an eval was one prompt, maybe two chained, with a simple judge. Agents that adjust to their own results make the number of paths to evaluate explode.S2 evolution
  • Context. Prompts grew from thousands of tokens to hundreds of thousands of tokens, so the team needed to search for specific tool calls and error codes inside huge traces.S2 search infra
  • Scale and languages. Over 100 million users, with a free trial of almost every AI product; and in Sarah's words, 60% of Notion's enterprise users "aren't speaking English", while every Notion AI engineer is.S4 03:33, 15:46
  • People. With 70 engineers working on AI, Braintrust's story says, "vibe checks" stopped working.S2 evolution

story · 03The loop: from feedback to a shipped fix

story · 04Finding the needle in a long trace

A trace is the record of one request: a tree of spans, one for each LLM call, tool call or retrieval step, with its inputs, outputs, tokens and timing. At Notion's scale, Sarah Sachs says in the Braintrust story, the hard part is often not fixing an error but finding it among all the data.S2 needle

The example the story gives is language. In a workspace where people write in Japanese, Korean and English, which language should the AI answer in? A wrong choice hits only some customers, so broad metrics miss it. Using Braintrust's search, the team built a dataset of every time language adherence failed, and added LLM judges so Notion AI wouldn't regress on it again. For some APAC customers, Sarah says, that eval was "probably one of the top improvements" in quality that year.S2 needle

Two details from Sarah's own talk explain how that works in practice:

  • A row can be one span. One AI interaction can involve about five LLM calls; the team can pull out just one of them and put it in a dataset.S4 16:39–17:33
  • Keep the request, re-run the rest. From a thumbs-down, what they keep is the user's request and the state of their workspace. The output gets regenerated by today's system, so a row from 2023 still tests the 2025 product.S4 18:27–19:20

Why exact search, and a database for it

For Notion, the Braintrust story says, trace search is often "not about semantic meaning" but about finding very specific items, fast, at scale. Notion's needs were part of why Braintrust built Brainstore, a database for LLM traces, and Notion was one of its first users.S2 search infra Braintrust's launch post (3 March 2025) describes logs growing fast across its customers, p95 log size rising from 500 KB to nearly 3 MB in a few months, with single spans often over 1 MB, which it puts at roughly 260,000 tokens. Brainstore keeps the data on object storage with a full-text index, and stores all the spans of a trace together.S3 volumes, design Its speed claims are Braintrust's own benchmarks, so we don't repeat them here; Sarah's quote in the post is that searches now take seconds that "would previously take hours".S3 testimonials

Cutting the bloat: Notion's own words

No source says traces alone shrank Notion's prompts. What Notion's leaders do describe is a habit and a fix:S5 09:22, 52:13

  • A weekly look at the worst case. Every Friday, Sarah says, the team sits down with the P99 of "the most token exhaustive" custom-agent transcript, works out why it didn't do well, and cuts tasks.
  • Show tools only when needed. With every tool in the prompt, Sarah says, just saying hello cost thousands of tokens, and each new niche tool made the model worse elsewhere. The fix was progressive disclosure: the agent searches for tools instead of seeing them all. Simon Last: "We're fighting to keep the prompt as short as possible," with over a hundred tools behind it.

story · 05Judges the Notion way

Sarah describes two common ways to use an LLM as a judge.S4 13:10–14:02

StyleWhat it looks likeTrade-off
One prompt for the whole dataset"Is this concise?" "Is this faithful?" run on every rowCheap to write; generic
One rule prompt per example"The answer should be in Japanese, the bullets formatted this way, and it should point to page A."More work to write; "far more insightful", and the style Notion's data specialists run

The per-example style has a second advantage for search. Notion's index changes all the time, so a stored "golden" answer goes stale. A rule like "the first result should be the most recent element in the list about the Q1 offsite" stays true when a newer document appears.S4 14:16 (You can try this in practice 02.)

Three more habits from the talk

  • Freeze retrieval. To tell retrieval bugs from generation bugs, they evaluate retrieval on its own, and evaluate generation on frozen retrieval results. When the answer is no longer in the index, the row is dropped.S4 24:36–25:27
  • Code where code works. Plenty of scorers are deterministic: if a dataset is about Jira questions, every search tool call in it must query Jira.S4 10:33
  • Don't over-trust one judge. Early judges written to check Notion's markdown formatting were relied on for everything, and missed answers that switched language. Sarah's fixes: one task per judge, or a small set of judges and a commitment to read the failures.S4 21:58–23:44
an honest admission

Asked whether judge scores are binary or a scale, Sarah said they use a scale she doesn't think is very well calibrated. They take everything below a threshold, people look at those outputs, and another LLM summarises the main failure themes into a report for the engineer.S4 19:20–20:12 Calibration research exists, she added, but it hadn't been worth the investment for them. analysis That works because humans still read the failures; if nobody does, an uncalibrated judge is a risk.

story · 06Three kinds of evals, three jobs

The Braintrust story splits Notion's evals into two kinds: regression evals, which catch breakage, and frontier evals, which show what a new model does differently. A new model "might pass a regression eval at 100%" like the last one; only the frontier eval says where it is better, so it can be deployed for those use cases first.S2 frontier

On the 2026 podcast Sarah gives the fuller picture, with three kinds and a target for each:S5 24:23

Why would anyone want evals that fail 70% of the time? Because Notion's evals had saturated: every new model passed, so the only feedback they could give was that it "wasn't worse". That told Notion nothing about where models were heading, and gave the labs nothing useful either. So over a few months, working with Anthropic and OpenAI, they built harder ones, and "Notion's Last Exam" now has a data scientist, a model behavior engineer and an evals engineer working on it full time.S5 24:23–25:56

story · 07A new model in under a day

Notion partners with several model providers and fine-tunes some of its own models. When a new model comes out, Sarah says, "usually less than a day" passes before all users have it in production.S4 04:27 The Braintrust story shows the flow as five steps:S2 frontier

  1. Run regression evals. Is anything worse?
  2. Run frontier evals. What can it do that the last model couldn't?
  3. Identify the model's differences.
  4. Deploy it for the specific use cases where it is better.
  5. Deploy within hours.

What makes that fast, in Notion's own words:

  • One button per model. Because evals are attached to each prompt, and each prompt can use a different model, the team can gather the high-volume tasks that don't need deep reasoning, run every eval on a fast, cheap new model "with one button", see which prompts it handles, and switch just those.S4 14:53
  • Feedback before launch. Labs send Notion several snapshots of a model before release, and Notion's feedback has changed which snapshot shipped.S5 23:30
  • Check the vendor too. The same model sold through different vendors sometimes shows different quality, and a regression can be acceptable if it buys latency where that matters.S5 22:59–23:30
  • A fallback for every prompt. Notion has hundreds of prompts. When a provider has an outage, moving all its traffic to one bigger model isn't viable: for database autofill it would cost about 12 times as much. So each prompt's owner sets its fallback in config ahead of time, and on-call just switches. Sarah wishes they had built it on day one.S4 26:19–28:04

New models also change the method itself: they can bring new kinds of reasoning and new parameters, so engineers restructure the evals for each one.S2 frontier

story · 08Who owns the evals

By 2026 the evals had become an organisation, not a tool:S5 21:40

  • A central framework, owned evals. A platform team maintains the eval framework; every product team owns its own evals. Many run in CI or nightly, and a custom agent alerts a team to its major failures.
  • A new job: model behavior engineer. It started as "data specialist", people with backgrounds like linguistics reading outputs in spreadsheets. Today they mostly build agents that write evals and LLM judges, and they triage failures. Sarah's view is that the job doesn't need an engineering background.S5 25:59
  • Evals as an agent harness. Simon Last describes making the eval system something a coding agent can drive end to end: download a dataset, run an eval, iterate on a failure, debug, implement a fix, with a human watching.S5 27:37
  • Product people read traces too. Reading thumbs-down data for its research mode showed people were trying to draft reports far more than they were doing research, which went to the product manager.S4 12:18 Braintrust's story adds that Notion builds its own review tools on top of the platform, such as a custom view where a data specialist labels data and adds it to the right eval set.S2 custom code

There was a change over time worth noticing. In June 2025 Sarah said they ran some evals before shipping "like an ad hoc CI" but did not wire them into CI, because the evals took too long.S4 11:26 By April 2026, regression evals "live in CI".S5 24:23

story · 10What we don't know

  • What an "issue" is in 3 → 30, over what period, and with how many people.
  • Any real eval score or judge-agreement number. The 30% and 80–90% figures are targets.
  • Notion's trace schema, data volumes or retention. Braintrust's log sizes are across all its customers.
  • How "under a day" is measured, and for which model launches.
  • How much the Friday reviews or progressive disclosure cut token use.
  • Which models serve which features, and which models act as judges.
claims you may see elsewhere

Summaries sometimes say Notion "debugs 100k+ token contexts" with full traces of "raw retrieval blocks" and "sub-agent state transitions", and that "structured trace trees" let developers "reduce prompt token bloat". The sources say prompts reached hundreds of thousands of tokens and that traces are searched for tool calls and error codes. They never mention retrieval blocks, state transitions or trace trees, and they don't credit traces with cutting tokens; the token savings Notion describes come from a weekly review and progressive tool disclosure. One database also dates Braintrust's Notion story to 2023; it was published on 9 October 2024. And "10x" is arithmetic on the 3 → 30 figure, not a reported metric.

practice · 03Wiring it into your code

A small tracer, an eval runner with three kinds of evals, per-example rule judges and a release report. It is our implementation, not Notion's or Braintrust's code, and it needs no account: traces and datasets are plain JSONL files. Any tracing tool, including the span tree in this module's previous case study, fits the same shape.

# pip install openai   (only for the adapter; the tests run offline)
import os

from openai import OpenAI

client = OpenAI()  # reads OPENAI_API_KEY from the environment


def make_llm(model: str):
    def llm(prompt: str) -> str:
        response = client.chat.completions.create(
            model=model,  # a chat model your account can use
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
            temperature=0,  # grading wants repeatable output
        )
        return response.choices[0].message.content or ""
    return llm


generator = make_llm(os.environ["GENERATOR_MODEL"])  # writes the answers you are testing
judge_llm = make_llm(os.environ["JUDGE_MODEL"])  # grades them: a different model family or version

1 · Record a trace

Each span keeps its parent's id, its tokens and its error, even when the step raises.

import itertools
import json
import time
from collections.abc import Iterator
from contextlib import contextmanager
from dataclasses import asdict, dataclass, field
from pathlib import Path
from typing import Any

@dataclass
class Span:
    id: str
    name: str
    kind: str  # "agent", "llm", "tool", "retrieval"
    parent: str | None = None
    input: Any = None
    output: Any = None
    tokens: int = 0  # tokens this span sent to a model (0 for plain code)
    ms: float = 0.0
    attrs: dict[str, Any] = field(default_factory=dict)
    error: str | None = None

class Tracer:
    """Records one request as a flat list of spans; each span keeps its parent's id."""

    def __init__(self, trace_id: str) -> None:
        self.trace_id = trace_id
        self.spans: list[Span] = []
        self._stack: list[str] = []
        self._ids = itertools.count(1)

    @contextmanager
    def span(self, name: str, kind: str, **attrs: Any) -> Iterator[Span]:
        parent = self._stack[-1] if self._stack else None
        s = Span(f"{self.trace_id}.{next(self._ids)}", name, kind, parent, attrs=attrs)
        self.spans.append(s)
        self._stack.append(s.id)
        start = time.perf_counter()
        try:
            yield s
        except Exception as exc:  # keep the error on the span, then let it propagate
            s.error = f"{type(exc).__name__}: {exc}"
            raise
        finally:
            s.ms = round((time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000, 2)
            self._stack.pop()

2 · Search it, find the bloat, make a dataset row

Search is exact text on purpose. The row keeps only the request; the system is re-run on it every time.

def save(spans: list[Span], path: Path) -> None:
    """Append spans to a JSONL file, one span per line."""
    with path.open("a", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for s in spans:
            f.write(json.dumps(asdict(s), ensure_ascii=False) + "\n")

def load(path: Path) -> list[Span]:
    return [Span(**json.loads(line)) for line in path.read_text(encoding="utf-8").splitlines() if line]

def walk(spans: list[Span]) -> Iterator[tuple[int, Span]]:
    """Depth-first walk of the tree rebuilt from parent ids: yields (depth, span)."""
    kids: dict[str | None, list[Span]] = {}
    for s in spans:
        kids.setdefault(s.parent, []).append(s)
    stack = [(0, s) for s in reversed(kids.get(None, []))]
    while stack:
        depth, s = stack.pop()
        yield depth, s
        stack.extend((depth + 1, c) for c in reversed(kids.get(s.id, [])))

def search(spans: list[Span], text: str) -> list[Span]:
    """Exact (case-insensitive) text search over everything a span holds: you are usually looking
    for one tool name, error code or phrase, not for something that means roughly the same."""
    needle = text.lower()
    return [s for s in spans if needle in json.dumps(asdict(s), ensure_ascii=False).lower()]

def heaviest(spans: list[Span], n: int = 3) -> list[Span]:
    """The spans that sent the most tokens: where to look first when a prompt bloats."""
    return sorted(spans, key=lambda s: s.tokens, reverse=True)[:n]

def to_case(span: Span, rules: list[str], kind: str) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Turn one span (say, the one LLM call that went wrong) into an eval row for a JSONL dataset.
    Only the request is kept; the system is re-run on it, so the row stays useful as the code changes."""
    return {"id": span.id, "input": span.input, "rules": rules, "kind": kind,
            "metadata": {"from_span": span.name, **span.attrs}}

3 · Cheap checks before any judge

Each case says which kind of eval it is and carries its own rules. Language and tool-target checks are plain code.

import json
from collections.abc import Callable
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from pathlib import Path
from typing import Any

LLM = Callable[[str], str]

KINDS = ("regression", "launch", "frontier")

@dataclass
class Case:
    id: str
    input: str
    rules: list[str]  # what a good answer must do, written for THIS example
    kind: str  # "regression" | "launch" | "frontier"
    checks: dict[str, str] = field(default_factory=dict)  # cheap code checks, e.g. {"language": "ja"}

def load_cases(path: Path) -> list[Case]:
    cases = [Case(**json.loads(line)) for line in path.read_text(encoding="utf-8").splitlines() if line]
    bad = [c.id for c in cases if c.kind not in KINDS or not c.rules]
    if bad:
        raise ValueError(f"cases need a known kind and at least one rule: {bad}")
    return cases

def language(text: str) -> str:
    """Rough script detector: enough to catch an answer in the wrong language."""
    letters = [ch for ch in text if ch.isalpha()]
    if not letters:
        return "unknown"
    ja = sum("぀" <= ch <= "ヿ" or "一" <= ch <= "鿿" for ch in letters)
    ko = sum("가" <= ch <= "힯" for ch in letters)
    share = max(ja, ko) / len(letters)
    return ("ja" if ja >= ko else "ko") if share >= 0.3 else "en"

def code_checks(case: Case, output: dict[str, Any]) -> list[str]:
    """Deterministic checks that need no model call. Returns the reasons it failed."""
    fails = []
    want = case.checks.get("language")
    if want and language(output["text"]) != want:
        fails.append(f"answer is in {language(output['text'])}, expected {want}")
    tool = case.checks.get("tool")
    if tool and not any(tool in q for q in output.get("tool_queries", [])):
        fails.append(f"no tool query targeted {tool}")
    return fails

4 · A rule judge per example

The judge sees the rules for this one request. A reply that isn't the JSON we asked for raises, and never counts as a pass.

RULE_PROMPT = """You grade one answer against rules written for this exact request.
Request: {input}
Answer: {answer}
Rules (every one must hold):
{rules}
Return ONLY JSON: {{"pass": true or false, "reason": "<the first rule that fails, or ok>"}}"""

class JudgeError(ValueError):
    """The judge replied with something that isn't the JSON we asked for."""

def rule_judge(llm: LLM, case: Case, answer: str) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    rules = "\n".join(f"- {r}" for r in case.rules)
    raw = llm(RULE_PROMPT.format(input=case.input, answer=answer, rules=rules))
    try:
        data = json.loads(raw)
        passed, reason = data["pass"], str(data["reason"])
    except (json.JSONDecodeError, KeyError, TypeError) as exc:
        raise JudgeError(f"unparseable verdict: {raw[:80]!r}") from exc
    if not isinstance(passed, bool):
        raise JudgeError(f"'pass' must be true or false, got {passed!r}")
    return passed, reason

5 · Run the evals

@dataclass
class Result:
    case_id: str
    kind: str
    passed: bool
    reasons: list[str]

def run_evals(cases: list[Case], system: Callable[[str], dict[str, Any]], judge: LLM) -> list[Result]:
    """Run the system on every case: code checks first, the rule judge only if they pass.
    A judge that answers in prose counts as a failure with a reason, never as a pass."""
    results = []
    for c in cases:
        output = system(c.input)
        reasons = code_checks(c, output)
        if not reasons:
            try:
                passed, why = rule_judge(judge, c, output["text"])
                reasons = [] if passed else [why]
            except JudgeError as exc:
                reasons = [str(exc)]
        results.append(Result(c.id, c.kind, not reasons, reasons))
    return results

def pass_rates(results: list[Result]) -> dict[str, float]:
    rates = {}
    for kind in KINDS:
        rs = [r for r in results if r.kind == kind]
        if rs:
            rates[kind] = sum(r.passed for r in rs) / len(rs)
    return rates

6 · The release report

Regression and launch evals gate; frontier evals never do. They report where the new model wins, and warn when they are saturating.

@dataclass
class Bars:
    regression: float = 0.95  # must hold, within your run-to-run noise (our default)
    launch: float = 0.80  # the launch "report card" bar; Notion mentions 80 or 90% of journeys
    frontier_saturated: float = 0.60  # frontier evals target ~30%; far above that, write harder ones

def release_report(old: list[Result], new: list[Result], bars: Bars | None = None) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Compare a candidate model with the current one. Regression and launch evals gate;
    frontier evals never gate, they say where the new model is different."""
    bars = bars or Bars()
    before = {r.case_id: r for r in old}
    broke = [r.case_id for r in new if r.kind != "frontier" and before[r.case_id].passed and not r.passed]
    wins = [r.case_id for r in new if r.kind == "frontier" and r.passed and not before[r.case_id].passed]
    rates = pass_rates(new)
    if rates.get("regression", 1.0) < bars.regression or broke:
        decision = "block"
    elif rates.get("launch", 1.0) < bars.launch:
        decision = "hold"
    else:
        decision = "ship"
    notes = []
    if rates.get("frontier", 0.0) > bars.frontier_saturated:
        notes.append("frontier evals are saturating: write harder ones")
    if decision == "ship" and not wins:
        notes.append("no frontier wins: nothing tells you where this model is better")
    return {"decision": decision, "rates": rates, "regressions": broke, "frontier_wins": wins, "notes": notes}

def pick_routes(old_by_prompt: dict[str, float], new_by_prompt: dict[str, float],
                margin: float = 0.02) -> dict[str, str]:
    """Per-prompt routing: switch a prompt to the new model only where its evals say so."""
    return {p: "new" if new_by_prompt.get(p, 0.0) >= old_by_prompt[p] + margin else "old"
            for p in old_by_prompt}

def missing_fallbacks(prompts: list[str], fallbacks: dict[str, str]) -> list[str]:
    """Every prompt owner pre-sets a fallback model, so on-call can switch during an outage."""
    return [p for p in prompts if not fallbacks.get(p)]

practice · 07When it breaks: symptoms, causes, fixes

Read a symptom, guess the cause, then open the card. analysis except where a card cites a source.

Likely cause. The judges were written for something else, such as formatting, and nobody reads their failures. Notion hit exactly this.S4 21:58

Fix. Search traces for the failures, build a language dataset, add a code check for the reply language and a judge for that one task.

Likely cause. Your evals are saturated: they can only say "not worse".S5 24:23

Fix. Write frontier evals the current model passes about 30% of the time, and keep them out of the release gate.

Likely cause. The index changed, and your evals compare against stored golden answers.S4 14:02

Fix. Rewrite them as rules ("the first result is the newest doc about X"), and drop rows whose answer left the index.S4 25:27

Likely cause. Every new tool definition and every full-page load goes into the prompt.

Fix. Review the most token-heavy traces every week, as Notion does, and show tools only when the agent asks for them.S5 09:22, 52:13

Likely cause. Nobody set a fallback per prompt, and one big model for everything is too expensive.S4 27:12

Fix. Each prompt's owner sets a tested fallback in config before any outage.

Likely cause. The "same" model can behave differently through different vendors.S5 22:59

Fix. Treat a vendor change like a model change: run the regression evals before switching.

practice · 08Rebuild the loop from memory

The six steps of the eval loop are shuffled. Put them in order, then check.

  1. Run the evals and check the targeted scores moved
  2. Pick one improvement: a new feature, a fix from feedback, or a better capability
  3. Iterate until you are happy, then ship and move on
  4. Tie the dataset to narrow scorers: code checks, rule judges, human review
  5. Check every other score for unintended regressions, and diff the experiments
  6. Curate a small targeted dataset: 10–20 rows from logs plus hand-written cases

wrap-upConclusion: make evals that move as fast as the models

Notion's story is less about a tool than about keeping evaluation useful as everything around it changes. Rare failures are found by searching traces and become dataset rows. Judges check rules written for each example, so they don't go stale. Regression, launch and frontier evals each have their own job and target. And because every prompt has its own evals and fallback, a new model can be judged, and shipped where it helps, within a day.

Four things to carry away

  • Make traces searchable by exact text; turn what you find into eval rows.
  • Write judges as rules for each example, not golden strings.
  • Run regression, launch and frontier evals, and gate only on the first two.
  • Know what your vendors wrote and what the company said; label both.

referenceGlossary

Trace / span
The record of one request (trace), made of timed steps (spans) such as LLM calls, tool calls and retrieval.
Regression eval
An eval for behaviour users already rely on; it must keep passing.
Launch report card
Evals for a feature being built, with a bar to clear before launch.
Frontier / headroom eval
An eval designed to be hard, passing about 30% of the time, to show what new models can do.
Saturated eval
An eval every model passes, so it can no longer tell models apart.
Rule judge
An LLM judge given the rules a good answer must meet for one specific example.
Golden answer
A stored correct answer that outputs are compared against.
Progressive disclosure
Showing an agent tools only when it needs them, instead of all at once in the prompt.
Model behavior engineer
Notion's name for people who write evals and judges and triage model failures.
Fallback model
The model a prompt switches to when its usual provider is down.
JSONL
A file with one JSON object per line; a common format for datasets and logs.
P99
The 99th percentile: here, the most token-heavy 1% of transcripts.
